HISTORY

How English came to be

English is a mixed language: Germanic at its core, with words from Latin, Old Norse, French and from all over the world. A short trip through 2,000 years – tap a word to hear it.

🇩🇪 Good to know if you speak German: because English grew out of Germanic languages, you’ll recognize many basic words right away – house/Haus, water/Wasser, bread/Brot, mother/Mutter.

  1. before 43 AD

    Influence: Celtic

    Celts in Britain

    Before there was English, people in Britain spoke Celtic languages – relatives of today’s Welsh. What stayed in English are mostly names of rivers and places.

  2. 43–410

    Influence: Latin

    The Romans

    The Romans ruled Britain for almost 400 years. Their Latin shaped towns and roads – many place names ending in “-chester” go back to the Latin “castra” (camp). Words like “street” (Latin strata) and “wall” (Latin vallum), however, had already been borrowed from Latin by the Germanic tribes on the continent.

  3. from about 450

    Influence: Germanic

    Angles, Saxons and Jutes: Old English

    Germanic tribes from today’s northern Germany and Denmark came to Britain. Their languages became Old English – that’s why English and German are closely related. The word “English” comes from the Angles.

    Fæder ūre, þū þe eart on heofonum … (Vaterunser auf Altenglisch / the Lord’s Prayer in Old English)

  5. from 793

    Influence: Old Norse

    The Vikings

    Vikings from Scandinavia raided England and settled in the north and east. Their Old Norse was so close to Old English that many everyday words were taken over – even “they”.

  6. 1066

    Influence: French

    The Normans: French arrives

    William the Conqueror won the Battle of Hastings. Afterwards the ruling class spoke French – to this day the animal often has a Germanic name (cow, pig), but the meat on the plate a French one (beef, pork).

  7. about 1100–1500

    Influence: English + French

    Middle English and Chaucer

    English became everyone’s language again – now full of French words. Around 1400, Geoffrey Chaucer wrote “The Canterbury Tales” and showed that great literature could be written in English.

    Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote … (Chaucer, „The Canterbury Tales“)

  8. about 1400–1700

    Influence: Sound change

    The Great Vowel Shift and printing

    The long vowels changed: “house” used to sound roughly like “hoos”, “bite” like “beet”. At the same time, William Caxton started printing books in England in 1476 – spelling became fixed before pronunciation had finished changing. That’s why English spelling and sound often don’t match.

    Influence: Latin and Greek

    The Renaissance, Shakespeare and the King James Bible

    Scholars brought in thousands of words from Latin and Greek. William Shakespeare (around 1600) made many words popular – for some, he is the earliest known written source – and the King James Bible of 1611 shaped the language for centuries.

  10. 1755 / 1828

    Influence: English

    Dictionaries: British and American

    Samuel Johnson published his great dictionary in 1755. In the US, Noah Webster’s dictionary of 1828 made simpler spellings popular – that’s why it’s “color” instead of “colour” and “center” instead of “centre”.
